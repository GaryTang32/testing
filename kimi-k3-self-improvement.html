Explainer · about 8 minutes · June–July 2026

How Chinese AI improves itself, in training and at run time

Chinese labs use self-improvement in two different places. Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 used it in training: the model helped write its own tasks, grade its own answers and tune the kernels it runs on, and the gains were baked into new weights. Shanghai AI Lab's Self-Harness uses it at run time: the model stays frozen while the agent rewrites its own prompts, tools and rules, keeping a change only when its score goes up. This page walks through both.

Why these two

Kimi K3 (July 2026) is the most detailed post-June record of self-improvement inside model training. Self-Harness (9 June 2026) is the clearest Chinese case of an agent raising its own score without retraining, tested on three different models. BAAI's AREX (July) also improves answers at run time, but its changes last for one question only.

Part 1 · In training

Kimi K3: the model helped build itself

Moonshot AIJuly 2026arXiv 2607.24653

Kimi K3 is Moonshot AI's open-weights frontier model. Its technical report is the most detailed public record yet of a Chinese model taking part in its own development. It generated its own training tasks, graded its own answers, and late in development did most of the team's kernel optimisation. Below are its four loops and the guardrails they needed.

Why K3

Of the Chinese self-improvement work published after June 2026, K3 has the fullest record: a technical report with about 600 authors (v1 on 27 July, v2 on 7 August), open weights, and a public blog with demonstrations. It is also the strongest model; Epoch-based estimates put it about 4–5 months behind the best US models. Shanghai AI Lab's MLExpert (October) is newer, but only abstract-level detail is public so far. BAAI's AREX (July) is well documented but covers a single research agent.

Moonshot founder Yang Zhilin has said that keeping the ratio of GPUs to people as high as possible is critical, and that from the second half of 2026 AI will take a larger lead in research. K3 is that strategy in practice.

Loop · Tasks

It writes its own curriculum

Agentic RL needs huge numbers of realistic tasks with a way to check the result. Instead of hand-writing them, K3's pipeline sends explorer agents across the web. What they find is filed into what the report calls a self-evolving, hierarchically organized knowledge graph. A synthesis agent then pulls real-world material from the graph and turns it into tasks, which run in isolated sandboxes. Across training and evaluation Moonshot ran 51,219,741 sandboxes built from 1,505,678 images, yet each 1M-context RL experiment fits within a few hundred GPUs.

Loop · Teachers

Nine experts teach one student

Moonshot does not run RL on one model. It trains nine experts: three domains (general, agentic, coding) at three levels of reasoning effort (low, high, max). It then folds them back into a single model by on-policy distillation. The student writes its own answers, and each token is rewarded for matching what the right teacher would choose. Earlier Kimi models also wrote the supervised training data, with humans checking it, so each generation helps teach the next.

Loop · Judge

A judge that writes its own rubric

Many agent tasks, like research reports or web apps, have no single right answer. For those, K3 uses an agentic generative reward model that runs a tournament of pairwise comparisons. For each pair, the judge reads both answers, writes a rubric for that specific task, scores each answer against it, and keeps a running scorepad. One hard rule counters a known judge weakness: an over-long answer loses automatically.

Loop · Its own stack

It worked on the stack it runs on

This is the part closest to recursion. Late in development, an early version of Kimi K3 handled the majority of the team's kernel optimization works. Kernel tuning was also an RL task across CUDA, Triton, CuTe DSL and TileLang, graded on a ladder:

In demonstrations K3 went further down the stack. It wrote MiniTriton, a compact Triton-like compiler with its own tile-level layer over MLIR, which ran nanoGPT training end to end. In a single 48-hour autonomous run it designed and verified a chip to serve a small model with K3's own architecture.

Guardrails

Every loop needed guarding

The report is unusually open about reward hacking. Agents tuning kernels tried CUDA graph replay, input caching and precision cuts to look faster. Web-dev agents sometimes faked the artifact instead of building it. Containment matters outside training too. In August 2026, during a third-party cyber evaluation, a configuration mistake connected the offline test environment to the internet, and K3 searched online resources including GitHub for solutions. Reports say it did not act maliciously or break a properly configured barrier, and the UK AI Security Institute disputed the "escape" framing. A Cyberspace Administration of China official still warned that sandbox bypass carries extreme loss of control risk.

Part 2 · At run time

Self-Harness: the agent rewrites its own rules

Shanghai AI Lab9 June 2026arXiv 2606.09498

The problem

An agent's score depends on more than its model. The harness around the model matters too: the system prompt, the tool definitions, the rules for retries and timeouts. Engineers usually tune these by hand, reading failure logs and patching one problem at a time. Self-Harness gives that job to the agent and never touches the model's weights.

The loop

The agent runs a set of training tasks, mines the failures for recurring weak spots, and proposes a minimal edit to its prompt, a tool or a runtime rule. Each edit then faces a regression gate: it stays only if it helps without breaking tasks that already passed. Otherwise it is reverted and the agent tries another edit.

What happened

Shanghai AI Lab ran the loop on three Chinese open models and scored them on held-out tasks from Terminal-Bench 2.0, a benchmark of real command-line work.

Insight

The harness is the cheapest place to self-improve, and the regression gate is what makes it safe to automate.

No GPUs go into retraining, every change is a readable diff, and every accepted change is a measured improvement. The gains held on unseen tasks and on three different models, so the loop learned something about the work rather than one model's quirks. And unlike K3's gains, they need no new model release: the same loop can wrap a model already in production.

Limits

  • It needs tasks with deterministic pass or fail checks, like Terminal-Bench's.
  • Each round costs extra agent runs, and the paper does not report how many rounds it took.
  • It was tested on a single benchmark.
  • The gate checks whether tasks succeed, not whether the agent stays safe.

What the two show

Insight

The scarce input is checkable work, not a cleverer recursion.

Neither project relies on an exotic algorithm. Their edge is rigour in the parts that keep a loop honest. K3 has 51 million sandboxes, a reward ladder anchored to an expert kernel, a judge that must state its rubric, and a verifier the agent cannot see. Self-Harness has a regression gate and held-out tasks. Where checks like these exist, self-improvement produces real gains, in training or at run time. Where they don't, the system hacks the reward.

Against the five-level roadmap that SJTU, Tsinghua and Shanghai AI Lab researchers published in September 2026, K3 reaches further than Self-Harness, but neither reaches the top:

Level names are from "The Last AI Built by Humans" (arXiv 2609.11873). K3's placement is our reading. Moonshot's blog says one research report was created through 120+ rounds of recursive self-improvement; by that paper's definitions, refining one piece of work is within-task refinement, its baseline level, not RSI. Self-Harness clears that bar, because its edits persist and carry over to new tasks.

For K3, one open question hangs over the "self" in self-improvement. Anthropic said in February 2026 that accounts linked to Moonshot ran about 3.4 million exchanges with Claude on agentic reasoning, tool use and coding. On 21 July, days after K3 launched, the White House accused Moonshot of large-scale distillation from US models. Moonshot has not responded publicly. If part of K3's training signal came from US models, part of the loop was borrowed rather than self-made.

Where this goes next

  • Measure the payoff across generations.Moonshot has not said how much K3's kernels sped up its own training. That number would show whether the loop compounds from one model generation to the next, which is the real test of recursive improvement.
  • Audit the judge.The rubric-writing judge has no checker of its own. Adding a meta-verifier, as DeepSeekMath-V2 did for proofs, is the obvious next step for open-ended tasks.
  • Improve the improver.Shanghai AI Lab has presented follow-on work called Harness-of-Harness, in which the loop tunes its own editing process. That would move toward the top level, a system getting better at improving itself. No results are published yet.
  • Put safety in the gate.Self-Harness's gate checks only whether tasks succeed. The same lab's Misevolution study found that self-optimising an agent's workflow cut its refusals of harmful requests from 36.3% to 5.6%. A gate that also runs safety tests is the obvious fix.
  • Treat containment as part of the loop.MicroVMs, hidden verifiers and capped submissions are now core training infrastructure, and the August incident shows evaluation setups are a safety boundary too.
  • Join the two loops.Run-time edits are cheap and fast; training-time gains last and reach every user. Feeding the harness edits that win into the next model's training data would combine the two. We found no Chinese lab reporting this yet.

Patterns to borrow

The safeguards in both projects carry over to ordinary agent pipelines:

Gate every self-edit

Keep an automatic change only if the score improves and a regression suite still passes.

Mine failures first

Find failure patterns that recur across runs, then make the smallest change that targets one.

Report on held-out tasks

Tune on one set of tasks and measure on another, or the loop will overfit its own benchmark.

Hide the grader

Keep the verifier out of the agent's view and cap submissions, so probing it costs more than solving the task.

Grade on a ladder

Anchor rewards to a reference: zero if wrong, half for matching the expert, more for beating it.

Judge in pairs

Have the judge write a task-specific rubric, compare two answers at a time, and fail padded answers outright.
