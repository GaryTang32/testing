AREX: the research agent that checks its own answers

AREX is a deep-research agent from the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence (BAAI), posted on 23 July 2026. It wraps an ordinary search-and-reason loop inside a second loop that audits each answer, constraint by constraint, and decides whether to accept it, fix it or start again. That outer loop adds about 10 points on BrowseComp, and helps a model with 10B active parameters score close to frontier systems many times its size.

Where it comes from

BAAI is a government-backed research institute in Beijing. At its annual conference on 12 June 2026 it ran a forum on AI self-evolution (AI 自进化论坛), with a roundtable titled 觉醒与进化：AI 如何自我迭代？ ("Awakening and evolution: how does AI iterate on itself?"). AREX followed six weeks later. The models are open on Hugging Face; training code is not mentioned in our notes.

The problem it attacks

Deep-research agents tend to fail the same way. They search, read and reason, then commit to an answer that fits most of the question but not all of it. BrowseComp, the benchmark AREX targets, is built to punish exactly that. Each of its 1,266 questions stacks several conditions that together point to one hard-to-find fact, so an answer that meets three conditions out of four is still wrong. One pass of research rarely gets every condition right.

Two loops, one inside the other

AREX keeps the usual research loop as its inner loop: search, read, reason, propose an answer. Around it sits an outer self-improvement loop. When the inner loop produces a candidate, the outer loop audits it against the question's constraints one at a time. It then takes one of three actions: accept the answer, refine it, or restart the research. It can take up to five such outer operations per question.

What an audit looks like

The audit works because a BrowseComp-style question breaks cleanly into separate conditions. Here is a made-up example to show the idea.

Illustrative example, written for this page

Name the research paper that:

  1. was published between 2018 and 2020
  2. has exactly three authors
  3. was presented at a conference held in a Nordic city
  4. thanks a museum in its acknowledgements
Round 1 · candidate "Paper A"
  • passpublished in 2019
  • passthree authors
  • passpresented in Stockholm
  • failthanks a library, not a museum

Refine. Three of four conditions hold, so look for a nearby candidate that also meets condition 4.

Round 2 · candidate "Paper B"
  • passpublished in 2018
  • passthree authors
  • passpresented in Oslo
  • passthanks a natural history museum

Accept. Every condition is satisfied, so return Paper B.

If most conditions had failed in round 1, patching would be pointless and the better move would be to restart. The paper's exact rules for choosing refine over restart are not in our notes; this example only shows the shape of the decision.

What the outer loop is worth

BAAI measured the pieces separately on BrowseComp. Starting from the inner loop alone, adding the outer self-improvement loop lifts the score by about 10 points, and adding what the paper calls context updating lifts it again to the full system's 82.5.

Is it recursive self-improvement?

Only partly, on the evidence we have. The roadmap paper "The Last AI Built by Humans" (SJTU, Tsinghua, Shanghai AI Lab and others, September 2026) draws a sharp line: refining your work within one task is its baseline level, B0, and does not count as RSI. Self-improvement only counts when changes persist and make the system better at future tasks.

The insight

Insight

Split one hard judgment into many small checks.

Asked "is this answer right?", a model tends to say yes. Asked "does this answer satisfy condition 3?", it can actually check. AREX's audit turns self-assessment, normally the weakest kind of verification, into a series of narrow questions that evidence can settle. A 2026 survey of 1,250 self-improvement papers found that gains track how grounded the check is, from formal proofs at the top to self-assessment at the bottom, and that ungrounded self-critique loses much of its effect over repeated rounds. Constraint-level audits are one way to move self-assessment up that ladder.

The restart branch matters as much as refine. It lets the agent abandon a bad line of research instead of patching it. Zhipu's chief scientist Tang Jie named exactly this as the hardest problem in self-evolution: 最大的问题是这个模型能自己判断自己什么时候停止，什么时候纠正自己 (the biggest problem is whether the model can judge for itself when to stop and when to correct itself). AREX gives that judgment an explicit, bounded place in the loop.

Limits

  • The ablations cover BrowseComp only, a benchmark whose questions are unusually easy to split into conditions.
  • There is no per-round curve, so we cannot tell whether round five adds as much as round one.
  • Every extra round costs more searches and time, and the cost per question is not in our notes.
  • The audit is still the agent judging itself. If it misreads a condition, it will "verify" the wrong thing with confidence.

Where this goes next

  • Carry the lessons forward.Train the next AREX on audited and corrected research trajectories, or keep a memory of failure patterns across questions. Either would move it from refining single answers to genuine self-improvement.
  • Publish the round-by-round curve.Most Chinese self-improvement loops gain little after two or three rounds. A curve over AREX's five operations would show whether its audit escapes that pattern.
  • Make the auditor independent.A separate checker, or a fresh evidence lookup for each condition, would catch the agent's own misreadings. DeepSeekMath-V2's meta-verifier is the model to copy.
  • Go beyond clean constraints.Open-ended research reports do not split into tidy conditions. The audit would need a rubric, and Kimi K3's judge, which writes its own rubric per task, suggests how.
  • Learn when another round is worth it.A cost-aware stopping rule would spend outer operations only where an answer is likely to change, keeping the gains at lower cost.

Patterns to borrow

AREX's design carries over to almost any agent that has to return a checkable answer:

Decompose, then verify

Turn the request into a checklist of conditions and check the answer against each, rather than asking "is this right?"

Give the loop three exits

Accept, refine or restart, with a hard cap on rounds so cost stays bounded.

Measure the outer loop on its own

Run with and without it, as BAAI did, so you know what the extra rounds actually buy.

Keep what you learn

Log which conditions fail most often across tasks. That is the step AREX, as recorded, leaves out.
